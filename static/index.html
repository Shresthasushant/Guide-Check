The problem

Why a buyer needs this before the auction, not after it.

You see a house advertised at $950,000. You like it. So you pay for a building inspection, a pest inspection and a conveyancer to read the contract — several hundred to a couple of thousand dollars, and a fortnight of your attention.

Then it sells for $1.4 million. You were never in the running. The money is gone and you start again on the next one.

This is common enough to be measured. On a single Saturday in NSW, an audit of 287 properties found half sold at least 10% above their guide. Consumer Affairs Victoria's underquoting taskforce has taken more than 8,800 complaints and issued more than 260 fines.

What the law actually asks

This is the distinction the whole tool is built on.

Not this question

“Did it sell for more than the guide?”

Properties sell above their guide all the time. Two determined bidders on a good day will push a price past any honest estimate. That is a hot auction, not misconduct, and it can only be known afterwards — far too late to help you.

This question

“Was the guide reasonable, given comparable sales?”

Under NSW law an agent's price guide must be a reasonable estimate, and the agent must be able to show it was based on evidence. That question can be asked before the auction — and comparable sales are public. So we ask it.

What Guide Check does

Three steps. It takes a few seconds.

  1. You describe the property and the guide

    A NSW suburb, the property type, the land size if you know it, and the advertised guide price. Nothing else is needed.

  2. It finds 25 comparable sales and adjusts them

    The 25 most similar recent sales nearby — same property type, close by, similar land size. No two properties are identical, so a model trained on 1.25 million NSW sales works out how much more or less your property is worth than each one, and adjusts its price accordingly.

  3. It shows where the guide sits among them

    If the guide sits below almost all of the adjusted sales, you see that — measured against how wrong the model typically is, so a small gap is not dressed up as a big one. And you see the sales themselves, with addresses, dates and prices.

What you get back

Every answer carries its own evidence and its own limits.

1

A plain verdict

One of four bands, from consistent with the comparable evidence to well below it — plus the percentile, so you get the honest continuous number rather than just a label.

2

The evidence

The comparable sales themselves: address, date, what they sold for, and what each one becomes once adjusted to your property. You can check them.

3

The limits

How often the model's range is actually right (77.6%, not the 80% it aims for), how old the comparable evidence is, and what the model cannot see.

What it is not

This is not an accusation of underquoting. Whether an agent underquoted depends on the agent's own reasonable estimate at the time of listing — something no price model can observe. Guide Check makes a statistical observation about a price against comparable sales. That is all it claims.

  • Not a valuation. A registered valuer inspects the property. This model has never seen it.
  • Not legal or financial advice.
  • Not street-level. The estimate is suburb-level: two properties of the same type and land size in one suburb get the same answer.
