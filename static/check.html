      How we arrived here

      01 Comparable sales Recent sales of the same property type near the subject.

      The comparables come from the NSW Valuer General Property Sales Information record — real, settled transactions, not listings or estimates. A nearest-neighbour index built over location, land area and property type returns the closest recent sales to the subject.

      02 Similarity filtering Only same-type sales inside the lookback window qualify.

      A house is never compared against a unit. Sales older than the lookback window are excluded, because a two-year-old price carries a market movement the adjustment cannot see. The subject's own sale, if it appears in the data, is excluded so a property is never compared against itself.

      Twenty-five comparables feed the distribution — far more than the five a human workpaper would show. A percentile computed from five numbers is too granular to mean anything.

      03 Adjustments Each sale is moved to what it would be worth as the subject.

      The model is not asked for a price. It is asked a narrower question it answers far better: how much more or less is the subject worth than this particular comparable? That ratio is applied to the comparable's real sale price.

      adjusted = sale_price × [ model(subject) ÷ model(comparable) ]

      Taking a ratio cancels most of the model's level error — if it is 8% low on the subject it is usually 8% low on a near-identical neighbour too. Adjustments are clipped at roughly half to double: beyond that the two properties are not really comparable and the adjustment cannot be trusted.

      04 Evidence comparison The guide is placed inside the adjusted distribution.

      The 25 adjusted values form an empirical distribution. The advertised guide is located inside it and reported as a percentile, not a yes/no flag — there is no arbitrary threshold, and the number degrades gracefully instead of falling off a cliff.

      05 Final analysis The gap is scaled by the measured error for this segment.

      A gap only matters relative to how wrong this tool usually is. The distance between the guide and the comparable median is divided by the model's measured typical error for properties of this segment, so a gap is called notable only when it is larger than the noise.

      That scaled figure, not the raw percentage, sets the band shown at the top of this report.
