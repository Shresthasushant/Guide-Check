Honest limits

What this cannot tell you

Every one of these is real and measured. None of them is hidden in a footnote — they appear on each result as well as here.

The biggest gap

The guide assessment has no validated precision or recall. We can show you that a guide sits well below the comparable evidence. We cannot yet tell you how often that judgement agrees with a human expert, because that needs a labelled set of real NSW listings — both genuinely underquoted ones and defensible ones that happened to sell high — and we have not collected it.

The scoring code for it is written and runs. The case file holds only a template row. Until it is filled, the band thresholds rest on the measured-error argument, not on measured agreement with human judgement. This is the largest open gap in the project and we are not disguising it.

Why the missing half matters more than you would expect: without genuine defensible-but-sold-high cases, the false-positive rate is unmeasurable. A tool that flags honest guides is worse than no tool, and we cannot currently prove this one does not.

Limits of the estimate

Each limitation is tagged by how much we actually know about it — measured, partly measured, or not measured at all. The tag is a word and a glyph, never a colour on its own.

  • Known

    The interval is wide, and slightly too narrow

    The 80% interval averages 78.5% of the sale price — honest, but not very informative on its own. That is why the guide assessment uses the comparable sales rather than this range.

    It covers 77.6% of cases, not the 80% it aims for. Slightly narrow, consistent with the geographic miscalibration documented in the conformal prediction literature. Every result displays the measured 77.6%, not the nominal figure.

  • Known

    The comparable sales are frozen

    The comparable-sales index ends at 30 September 2025, deliberately, so it can never contain a sale from the test set used to measure accuracy. The evidence therefore ages as the tool is used.

    Every result reports the cutoff and how many months past it the valuation date sits. In a rising market, treat the estimate as a floor on today's evidence — and rebuild the index against fresher data before relying on it.

  • Limited

    It is suburb-level, not street-level

    The source data has no coordinates, only address text, so every sale is resolved to its suburb centroid. For a property you type in, everything except type, postcode, land area and date comes from the most recent recorded sale in that suburb.

    Two properties of the same type and land size in one suburb get the same answer. A waterfront position, a main-road frontage, a renovated kitchen and a derelict bathroom are all invisible.

  • Limited

    Land area is missing more often than you would think

    Land area is absent on 23% of residential sales, and on 60% of units, where the fallback is the median land size for the type. It is the strongest structural signal the model has, so a missing value measurably widens the answer.

    Supply it from the listing whenever you can.

  • Unknown

    No bedrooms, bathrooms or condition

    NSW sales data contains no bedroom or bathroom counts. The form records them and the result says plainly that they were not used. They are the single largest missing feature — collecting them from live listings is what would let a future model use them.

    Condition, renovation, aspect and view are not in the data in any form. Two identical blocks can differ by a third on these alone.

  • Unknown

    Whether this tool agrees with a human expert

    The guide assessment has no validated precision or recall. The scoring code is written and runs; the labelled case file holds only a template row. Until it is filled, the band thresholds rest on the measured-error argument, not on measured agreement with human judgement.

    This is the largest open gap in the project, restated at the top of this page.

Limits of the question

Even a perfect model would not answer the question people most want answered.

  • Underquoting depends on the agent's own estimate. Under NSW law the test is whether the guide was less than the agent's reasonable estimate of the likely selling price. That estimate lives in the agent's records, not in the sales data. No price model can observe it.
  • Selling above the guide is not misconduct. Two determined bidders will push a price past any honest estimate. This tool deliberately does not treat “sold high” as evidence of anything.
  • A defensible guide can still be a low one. Agents may quote toward the bottom of a supportable range. That is a strategy, not a breach, and the tool will correctly stay quiet.
  • The market moves. A guide that was defensible when set may look low three months later. The tool judges against evidence up to the index cutoff, not against the market on auction day.

How to use it responsibly

  • Read the comparables, not just the band. If the five nearest sales are obviously unlike the property you are looking at, the answer is weak no matter what the badge says.
  • Treat “well below” as a reason to ask a question, not as a conclusion. A good question is: which comparable sales support this guide?
  • Do not repeat the output as an accusation. It is a statistical observation about a price against comparable sales. Saying more than that is not supported by anything on this site.
  • For a decision that matters, get a valuation. A registered valuer inspects the property. This model has never seen it.

Guide Check is not legal, valuation or financial advice, and is not a substitute for professional advice or your own inspection of a property.
